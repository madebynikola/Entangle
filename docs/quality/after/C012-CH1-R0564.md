## Ontology-Driven Scanner Integration to Cut Pentest Engagement Prep from Four Days to One

**Company:** Sentinel Byte preduzetnik works in cybersecurity. [C012] Challenge C012-CH1.  
**Researcher:** Árpád Nagy is a Full Professor in the Department of Computing and Control Engineering, Faculty of Technical Sciences. [R0564]  
**Format:** joint project, one page.

### The problem
A manual penetration test engagement takes about two weeks, with four days spent on reconnaissance and vulnerability enumeration before testing can begin. [C012-CH1] One client has asked for something closer to monthly checks on their external attack surface, and the company does not have the hours to bill that work at current rates and stay profitable. [C012-CH1] The company's in-house Bash scripts save about two hours per engagement but break when Nessus changes its output format, which has happened twice in recent years. [C012-CH1, C012/blog/2024-04-02/Why we wrote our own reconnaissance scripts and why we are about to stop] Attempts to add a diff step to the scripts produce enough noise that testers must still review everything manually before trusting the output. [C012/blog/2024-04-02/Why we wrote our own reconnaissance scripts and why we are about to stop]

### Research questions
1. Can a normalisation layer built on OWL 2 mappings parse Nmap, Nessus, and Burp Suite outputs in a format-stable way, measured by zero parse failures across three consecutive Nessus version upgrades on the company's historical scan archive? 
2. Does structured change detection on the normalised data reduce tester review time for a diff report to under one working day, measured against the current four-day baseline on five real client engagement datasets?

### Approach
- Apply the ontology-driven normalisation method from Nagy's prior work — OWL 2 class definitions and SPARQL mapping rules — to ingest Nmap XML, Nessus .nessus, and Burp Suite XML outputs into a single structured finding store, isolating the pipeline from upstream format changes.
- Design a change-detection layer that compares successive normalised finding stores and classifies each delta as new, resolved, or changed, using the false-positive and credentialed-scan benchmarks from Nagy's scanner evaluation work to set per-finding confidence scores that suppress low-confidence noise.
- Implement a scheduler that triggers reconnaissance and enumeration runs against client-defined scopes on a configurable interval, using hybrid active-probing patterns studied in Nagy's reconnaissance work, and produces a structured Markdown report from the delta store for direct handoff to testers.

### First six months
- Week 1–2: The company engineer exports all historical Nmap and Nessus scan files into a shared project repository; the master's student sets up a local environment and inventories format versions present in the archive.
- Week 3–6: The student drafts OWL 2 class definitions for Nmap and Nessus fields; Nagy reviews the ontology design; the company engineer annotates five historical engagement pairs to serve as ground truth for the diff evaluation.
- Month 2: The student runs a parse-failure audit across all archived format versions and reports the failure count; the team agrees on the confidence-score thresholds to use in change detection.
- Month 3: The student begins Burp Suite XML mapping and the change-detection module; the company engineer allocates roughly two days per week to test outputs against real tester expectations.

### Why this pair
The company's core difficulty — scanner output that breaks on version changes and produces untrustworthy diffs — is precisely the heterogeneous-feed normalisation and false-positive-suppression problem that Nagy's published work addresses. Neither side could solve it alone: the company brings the real scan archive, the ground-truth engagement data, the live client scopes, and the operational knowledge of what testers actually need; Nagy brings the ontology design methods and the scanner benchmarking evidence needed to set defensible confidence thresholds. The budget and six-month scope fit a master's student from Nagy's group placed at the company, with the company engineer's two days per week keeping the work grounded in real tester workflow.

### Agreed up front (proposed)
- Publication: the company has a 45-day review window before any paper or technical report is submitted; findings that reveal client-identifying details are redacted or generalised before submission.
- Ownership: the company owns the deployed prototype, the trained mapping rules, and all configuration files; the university retains the OWL 2 schema and SPARQL mappings as reusable research artefacts and may publish the method.
- Data access: the student works on company scan data under a signed non-disclosure agreement; data does not leave the company's infrastructure or the agreed project repository, and is deleted from university systems at project close.
- Preconditions: client scopes used in the scheduler trial must have written consent from the client before any live scan is run; the project starts only after the NDA is signed by both parties.
