## Unsupervised autoencoder anomaly detection for Modbus and BACnet/IP on embedded appliances

**Company:** ArmaturaSec d.o.o. works in cybersecurity. [C001] Challenge C001-CH1.  
**Researcher:** Vuk Đukić is an Associate Professor in the Department of Computing and Control Engineering, Faculty of Technical Sciences. [R1015]  
**Format:** industrial PhD, three years. The researcher is the academic supervisor; the company names an industrial supervisor and employs the candidate.

### The problem
A supervised classifier trained on public ICS datasets produced unacceptable false-positive rates when deployed on real client Modbus and BACnet/IP networks, making supervised detection infeasible for the company's operational environment. [C001-CH1, C001/blog/2024-05-20/Why We Stopped Trying to Use Public ICS Datasets for Anomaly Detection] ArmaturaSec has zero confirmed malicious traffic samples from its client sites, so any detection method must learn baseline behaviour from normal traffic alone. [C001-CH1] ArmaturaSec intends to support on-device anomaly detection without affecting packet forwarding latency, and the company states it has the hardware to run inference on-device. [C001, C001-CH1] Plant manager customers need alerts explained in plain language, not a black-box score. [C001-CH1]

### Research questions
1. Can an autoencoder trained only on site-specific normal Modbus TCP and BACnet/IP traffic detect behavioural anomalies with a false-positive rate acceptable in production deployment? Measured as FPR and detection rate against labelled synthetic fault and replay-attack injections on the company's hardware-in-the-loop test bench.
2. Can model-compression techniques (quantisation and pruning) reduce an autoencoder trained on Modbus and BACnet/IP traffic to fit the appliance's embedded hardware while keeping reconstruction-error thresholds stable? Measured as model size, inference latency on target hardware, and change in FPR relative to the uncompressed model.
3. Can reconstruction-error attribution over named protocol fields produce alert descriptions that non-technical plant managers correctly interpret as actionable? Measured by a structured comprehension test with plant manager participants drawn from consenting client sites.

### Approach
- Train a per-site autoencoder on normal-traffic captures of Modbus TCP function codes and register sequences, using the reconstruction-error approach from the researcher's published work on Modbus anomaly detection. Extend the same architecture to BACnet/IP object and service sequences as a new protocol contribution.
- Apply quantisation and pruning techniques from the researcher's published work on resource-constrained IoT intrusion detection to compress the trained models to fit the appliance's embedded processor without packet-forwarding latency impact; validate latency and accuracy on the company's hardware-in-the-loop test bench.
- Design an explainability layer that maps high reconstruction error back to named protocol fields (e.g. unexpected function code, register value out of learned range, abnormal polling interval) and generates a one-sentence plain-language alert; evaluate comprehension with plant manager participants from consenting client sites.
- Evaluate the full pipeline end-to-end on held-out site captures and on injected synthetic anomalies (replay attacks, command injection, abnormal polling patterns) on the test bench, comparing detection rate and FPR across Modbus and BACnet/IP before and after compression.

### First six months
- Month 1–2: The company transfers a representative subset of its normal-traffic packet captures (Modbus TCP and BACnet/IP, from at least two distinct site types) to a secured research environment under NDA. The researcher and the company engineer agree on a common feature representation for each protocol.
- Month 2–3: The candidate, with the company engineer, documents the appliance's embedded hardware constraints (processor, memory, latency budget) and establishes a performance baseline by profiling a reference model on the test bench.
- Month 3–5: The candidate trains initial autoencoders on the Modbus TCP captures and evaluates reconstruction-error thresholds against the normal-traffic baseline; the researcher reviews architecture choices against findings from the published Modbus anomaly detection work.
- Month 5–6: The candidate and company engineer inject the first synthetic anomalies (replay attacks and abnormal polling intervals) on the hardware-in-the-loop bench and record detection rate and FPR; results go into a milestone report shared with both supervisors.

### Agreed up front (proposed)
- Publication: the company may review any manuscript for up to 60 days before submission; the company may request removal of client-identifying network topology details but may not block publication of methods or aggregate results.
- Ownership: the company owns the trained models, compressed firmware, and the labelled evaluation dataset; the University of Novi Sad retains the right to use the methods and anonymised experimental results for teaching and further research.
- Data access: the company transfers traffic captures to a secured research environment under a mutual NDA signed before month 1; raw captures remain confidential and are deleted from research systems at project end or on written request.
- Preconditions: client consents for use of captured traffic in research must be confirmed in writing by the company before data transfer; the project starts only after these consents are in place.
