## Structured Reconnaissance Pipeline with Incremental Change Detection for Penetration Testing Engagements

**Company:** Sentinel Byte preduzetnik works in cybersecurity. [C012] Challenge C012-CH1.  
**Researcher:** Árpád Nagy is a Full Professor in the Department of Computing and Control Engineering, Faculty of Technical Sciences. [R0564]  
**Format:** joint project, one page.

### The problem
Clients including two municipalities and a medical supply company are requesting continuous or monthly testing, which the company cannot meet within its current billing model. [C012-CH1] The current automation consists of Bash scripts wrapping Nmap and other tools that break when tool versions change and were built for single runs, not comparison across time. [C012/blog/2024-04-02/Why we wrote our own reconnaissance scripts and why we are about to stop] The diff step between successive scans produces too much noise and requires manual review before output can be trusted. [C012/blog/2024-04-02/Why we wrote our own reconnaissance scripts and why we are about to stop] The company plans to offer continuous monitoring as a service within the next twelve months, which requires reducing the initial engagement phase from four days to one day. [C012, C012-CH1] Árpád Nagy led vulnerability enumeration work in the SECSME project and conducted paid pilot assessments for two technology companies at NTP Novi Sad under the GRAPHSEC project. [SYN-PRJ-0507, SYN-PRJ-0508, R0564]

### Research questions
1. Can an agent-based passive-active probing pipeline, wrapping Nmap and Nessus, reduce the reconnaissance and enumeration phase from four days to one day per engagement — measured by wall-clock time on five anonymised re-runs of past client scopes against the company's historical baseline?
2. Can an incremental change-detection algorithm, applied to successive scan snapshots, produce a structured diff (new hosts, changed ports, new or resolved findings) with fewer false-positive change events than the current Bash diff — measured as the share of flagged changes that require no manual correction, against a reviewer-annotated ground truth built from six months of historical scan pairs?

### Approach
- Model each scan session as a structured snapshot using the vulnerability data normalisation and ontology-driven schema from Nagy's prior work, replacing the current flat text files with a versioned, schema-validated store that Nmap and Nessus output can be loaded into directly.
- Apply the passive-active probing fusion architecture from Nagy's network reconnaissance research to the scheduling layer: passive observation anchors host liveness before active Nmap sweeps fire, cutting redundant probe volume and stabilising host inventory across scan runs.
- Implement the incremental update algorithm from Nagy's attack graph work as the change-detection engine: the algorithm recomputes only the changed subgraph between two snapshots rather than diffing raw text, so the output is a typed change set (host added, port closed, CVE resolved) instead of noisy line diffs.
- Use the scanning detectability and scan-parameter optimisation work to tune Nmap timing and probe selection so that scans against municipal and SME clients do not trigger their perimeter monitoring, reducing scan-induced incident tickets.
- Define a structured hand-off schema — hosts, open ports, service banners, Nessus finding IDs, severity, and delta type — that the pipeline writes to a format testers can load directly into their working notes, completing the four-day-to-one-day reduction goal.

### First six months
- Week 1–2: the company exports all existing Bash scripts and two years of scan output (anonymised) to a shared, access-controlled repository. Nagy's group reviews them and produces a gap analysis against the normalised schema design.
- Week 3–4: both sides agree on the snapshot schema fields — host, port, service, Nessus finding ID, severity, scan timestamp — and on the three historical scopes to use as the baseline benchmark.
- Month 2: set up the test environment on the company's Kali Linux infrastructure. Run the existing scripts against the three historical scopes and record phase durations and raw diff line counts as the baseline numbers the prototype must beat.
- Months 3–4: implement the snapshot ingestion module and the incremental change-detection engine. Load the three historical scope pairs and produce typed change sets. Compare to the raw diffs and manually annotate a sample of 200 change events to begin the ground-truth dataset.
- Month 5: integrate Nmap and Nessus scheduling with the passive-active probing layer. Run the end-to-end pipeline on the company's own test infrastructure and measure wall-clock time for the first time on a full scope.

### Why this pair
Sentinel Byte has the data, the toolchain, and the operational context to validate a pipeline, but lacks the algorithmic research background to move from fragile text-diffing to a principled, schema-driven change-detection engine. Árpád Nagy has published the exact methods needed — passive-active probing fusion, incremental graph update algorithms, and vulnerability data normalisation — but has no access to real engagement scan histories, live client scopes, or the Nessus-plus-Kali operational environment the pipeline must run in. Neither side could build and validate the full artefact alone: the company would produce another fragile script, and the researcher would produce a prototype with no real-world data or deployment path.

### Agreed up front (proposed)
- Publication: either party may submit results for publication after a 30-day review window during which the company may request redaction of client-identifying details or operationally sensitive scan parameters. The university retains the right to publish after the review window closes.
- Ownership of code and pipeline: the complete pipeline, deployment scripts, and schema definitions are jointly owned. The company holds an irrevocable licence to use, modify, and deploy the pipeline for commercial engagements. The university retains the right to use the methods and architecture in future research.
- Ownership of methods: the normalisation schema, change-detection algorithm design, and probing architecture remain available for academic use by the researcher. Neither party may transfer exclusive commercial rights to a third party without written consent of the other.
- Data access: historical scan output and any live engagement data used in the project are provided by the company under a data-use agreement specifying that the university team may use the data only for this project. All client-identifying information is anonymised before transfer to the university.
- Precondition — data protection: because scan data may include information about third-party (client) systems and personnel, both parties must agree in writing on anonymisation procedures and data retention limits before any historical data is transferred. The agreement must comply with GDPR and the Serbian Law on Personal Data Protection.
- Budget and invoicing: the company's contribution of 3,000 to 5,000 euros covers direct project costs (tooling licences, test infrastructure, travel for joint sessions). A detailed cost plan is agreed before month 1 begins, with no expenditure above the ceiling without written approval.
- Scope control: the project delivers a working prototype and validation results. Productionisation, client-facing deployment, and ongoing maintenance beyond the ten-month period are out of scope and subject to a separate agreement.
