## Unsupervised Modbus TCP Anomaly Detection with Threshold Calibration for On-Device Deployment

**Company:** ArmaturaSec d.o.o. works in cybersecurity. [C001] Challenge C001-CH1.  
**Researcher:** Krisztina Kiss is a Full Professor in the Department of Computing and Control Engineering, Faculty of Technical Sciences. [R1008]  
**Format:** joint project, one page.

### The problem
ArmaturaSec has several terabytes of normal Modbus TCP and BACnet/IP traffic captures collected over three years from consenting clients, and zero confirmed malicious traffic. [C001-CH1, C001] Supervised classifiers trained on public ICS datasets produce false positive rates too high for production deployment at ArmaturaSec's client sites. [C001-CH1, C001/blog/2024-05-20/Why We Stopped Trying to Use Public ICS Datasets for Anomaly Detection] ArmaturaSec requires results to be explainable in plain language to plant managers, not a black box. [C001-CH1] ArmaturaSec plans a fifth hardware variant with a dedicated security processor to support anomaly detection without affecting packet forwarding latency. [C001]

### Research questions
1. Can a one-class model trained solely on ArmaturaSec's site-specific normal Modbus TCP captures detect injected anomalies at a false positive rate below the threshold that makes production deployment viable? Measured by precision, recall, and false positive rate on a held-out normal segment plus a synthetic anomaly set injected in the testbed, compared against the false positive baseline that public-dataset-trained classifiers currently produce in production.
2. Can threshold calibration using beta mixture models reduce the gap between nominal and actual false positive rates across at least three distinct client site profiles in ArmaturaSec's corpus? Measured by the calibration error (difference between target and observed false positive rate) before and after calibration on each site's held-out traffic segment.
3. Can weight quantization and structured pruning compress the best-performing model to run inference within acceptable latency bounds on the resource-constrained gateway hardware ArmaturaSec already deploys? Measured by inference latency in milliseconds and detection performance drop versus the uncompressed model on the same test set.

### Approach
- Extract per-flow and per-session feature vectors from ArmaturaSec's Modbus TCP captures using statistical traffic characterisation methods covering periodicity, write-to-read ratios, coil change frequency, and inter-write interval statistics — features established in Kiss's published ICS traffic work — without requiring a protocol parser.
- Train one-class SVM, Isolation Forest, autoencoder, and Gaussian mixture model baselines on the normal-traffic-only corpus, following the same unsupervised protocol used in Kiss's ICS anomaly detection publications, to identify which model family best fits ArmaturaSec's traffic structure.
- Apply beta mixture model threshold calibration to the winning detector across multiple client site profiles from ArmaturaSec's corpus, using the calibration procedure from Kiss's published work on aligning nominal and actual false positive rates under imbalanced operating conditions.
- Inject synthetic attack scenarios (replay, command injection, write flooding) via Kiss's ICS testbed traffic generators into a copy of site captures to produce a labelled evaluation set; evaluate the calibrated detector against this set and against ArmaturaSec's engineer's manual reviews.
- Apply weight quantization and structured pruning to compress the calibrated model and benchmark inference latency and detection performance on hardware matching ArmaturaSec's existing gateway specification.

### First six months
- Week 1–2: Sign data-sharing agreement and NDA covering ArmaturaSec's traffic corpus and firmware codebase; agree on the three client site profiles to be used in the study and confirm anonymisation requirements.
- Week 3–4: ArmaturaSec transfers the first 500 GB Modbus TCP capture subset to Kiss's group via a secure, agreed channel; Kiss's group confirms receipt, checksums, and stores data in an access-controlled environment.
- Month 2: Kiss's group runs statistical traffic characterisation on the transferred captures; ArmaturaSec's engineer with anomaly detection background reviews the resulting feature set and flags any operationally implausible features.
- Month 3: Kiss's group sets up the four candidate one-class models on the characterised feature set; ArmaturaSec engineer attends a half-day session at Kiss's lab to see the testbed traffic generator and agree on the synthetic anomaly scenarios to inject.
- Month 4: First model training runs complete; Kiss's group shares preliminary detection results with ArmaturaSec; both sides agree on the evaluation metric targets (target false positive rate, latency budget) that will govern model selection in month 4.

### Why this pair
ArmaturaSec has a unique and large corpus of site-specific normal ICS traffic but no labelled attack data and no in-house research capacity to build a production-grade unsupervised detector from it. Kiss has published the specific methods — one-class models, beta mixture calibration, model compression for ARM gateways — that map directly onto each of ArmaturaSec's named requirements. Neither side could do this alone: ArmaturaSec lacks the detection methods and research infrastructure, while Kiss's group has no access to multi-site, real-world Modbus TCP captures at this scale, nor to the deployment hardware and client context needed to validate a production-ready system.

### Agreed up front (proposed)
- Publication: either party may submit results for publication after a 60-day review period during which ArmaturaSec may request redaction of commercially sensitive client details. Neither party may unreasonably withhold consent beyond this period.
- Ownership of results: ArmaturaSec owns the trained and compressed model artefacts and the integration documentation. The University of Novi Sad retains the right to use the methods, algorithms, and non-client-identifying findings for future research and teaching.
- Data access: ArmaturaSec's traffic captures remain ArmaturaSec's property throughout. Kiss's group stores them in an access-controlled environment, uses them only for this project, and deletes or returns them at project close. All client-site data is anonymised before transfer under a procedure agreed in writing before month 1.
- Code: the feature extraction pipeline, training scripts, calibration code, and compression scripts are jointly owned. Both parties receive a copy. Neither may sublicense to a third party without written consent of the other.
- Precondition — data-sharing agreement: a signed data-sharing and NDA agreement covering ArmaturaSec's traffic corpus and firmware codebase must be in place before any data is transferred. The project start date is contingent on this agreement being executed.
- Precondition — client consent: ArmaturaSec confirms in writing before transfer that all traffic captures used in the project were collected under client consent that permits use in collaborative research.
