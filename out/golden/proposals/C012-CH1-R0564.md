## Agent-Based Scanning Pipeline to Cut Penetration-Test Reconnaissance from Four Days to One

**Company:** Sentinel Byte preduzetnik works in cybersecurity. [C012] Challenge C012-CH1.  
**Researcher:** Árpád Nagy is a Full Professor in the Department of Computing and Control Engineering, Faculty of Technical Sciences. [R0564]  
**Format:** joint project, one page.

### The problem
Each penetration-test engagement takes about two weeks, with a few days spent on reconnaissance and vulnerability enumeration; clients are requesting continuous testing and retesting after patches, which the company cannot sustain at current billing rates without automation. [C012-CH1] Existing Bash scripts break when tool versions change — Nessus has changed its output format twice since the scripts were written — and the diff step for detecting changes between scans requires manual review to manage noise from edge cases. [C012/blog/2024-04-02/Why we wrote our own reconnaissance scripts and why we are about to stop] Multiple real clients — including two municipalities and a regional medical supply company — are asking for continuous or frequent retesting, but the company cannot offer retest work without adding staff unless the initial phase is cut from four days to one. [C012-CH1]

### Research questions
1. Can an agent-based pipeline complete host discovery, service enumeration, and vulnerability data collection on a target network and deliver a structured JSON report within one working day, measured against the company's current four-day baseline on three anonymised past engagement scopes?
2. Does an incremental change-detection step between successive scans reduce manual review time compared with the company's current diff approach, measured by the number of false-positive change alerts on a controlled test network with known-good and known-changed states?

### Approach
- Apply the agent-based scanning architecture from the researcher's host-discovery and topology-mapping work to orchestrate Nmap and Nessus scans against target network scopes, replacing the fragile Bash wrapper with a versioned, tool-agnostic coordination layer.
- Use the ontology-driven normalisation and SPARQL integration method from the vulnerability data normalisation work to ingest Nessus and Nmap output into a unified internal representation that is insulated from tool version changes.
- Implement the incremental graph-update method from the researcher's attack graph work to detect and classify changes between scans — new hosts, closed ports, new or patched CVEs — with a noise threshold tuned on the company's test environment.
- Apply the LLM-assisted structured extraction method to convert the unified internal representation into the JSON report schema the company's manual testers receive, including suggested remediation priorities.

### First six months
- Month 1: The company transfers three anonymised past engagement scopes (target lists, historical Nmap and Nessus output) to the researcher's laboratory under a signed data-handling agreement; the researcher sets up a matching network topology in the VLAN emulation lab.
- Month 2: A student intern from the researcher's laboratory builds the agent-based coordination layer and the ontology-driven normalisation module; Nikola Kovačević dedicates roughly two days per week to the project, primarily on tooling and automation topics.
- Month 3: Baseline timing runs on all three scopes; the team agrees on the JSON report schema with the company's manual testers and sets the noise threshold target for change detection.
- Months 4–6: The student implements and calibrates the incremental change-detection module and LLM report generator; the company engineer validates each module on a live retest engagement; the pair reviews results and prepares the technical note.

### Why this pair
Sentinel Byte has a concrete, measurable bottleneck — four days of fragile, manual-adjacent work — and real clients ready to consume a faster service, but lacks the methods to build a reliable, version-stable pipeline with principled change detection. Árpád Nagy brings published, tested methods for exactly the four sub-problems that make up that bottleneck: agent-based orchestration, normalisation across tool versions, incremental change detection, and structured report generation. Neither could reach the target alone: the company brings the engagement data, the live toolchain, and the practitioner judgement needed to validate the prototype against real pentesting work; the researcher brings the laboratory infrastructure, student internship capacity, and the published methods that address the fragile-script problems.
