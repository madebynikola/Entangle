## Response-Surface Optimisation of Slow Pyrolysis to Reach ≥70% Fixed Carbon Without Sacrificing Yield

**Company:** Suncokret Eko d.o.o. works in cleantech — biomass and organic waste valorisation. [C007] Challenge C007-CH1.  
**Researcher:** Đorđe Vukelić is an Associate Professor in the Department of Applied and Engineering Chemistry, Faculty of Technology. [R0992]  
**Format:** joint project, one page.

### The problem
The reactor operates at 500–520°C and produces biochar with around 55% fixed carbon and roughly 28% mass yield, but German and Dutch buyers require at least 70% fixed carbon. [C007-CH1] Raising operating temperature to 650°C or above reaches ≥70% fixed carbon but drops mass yield below 20%, making the economics unviable. [C007-CH1] The company has two years of batch logs covering temperature profiles, feedstock moisture, particle size and output quality on the existing slow-pyrolysis reactor. [C007-CH1]

### Prior work
- A 2022 study reports slow pyrolysis of corn cob at 600°C with characterised biochar yields and proximate analysis, showing elevated inorganic nutrient content favourable for soil application. [W4302028689]
- A 2025 review reports that lower temperatures and longer residence times enhance biochar yield and carbon content, while higher temperatures favour gas and liquid production. [W4413824496]

### Research questions
1. What combination of temperature (500–650°C), residence time, and particle size achieves ≥70% fixed carbon in sunflower-husk and maize-cob biochar while keeping mass yield at or above 20% on the existing reactor? Measured by proximate analysis of outputs from a response-surface trial set run on the company's reactor, validated against European Biochar Certificate criteria.
2. Do sunflower husks and maize cobs require different process windows, or does a single set of conditions satisfy ≥70% fixed carbon and ≥20% yield for both feedstocks? Measured by comparing RSM model optima and confirmatory batch results for each feedstock against EBC criteria.

### Approach
- Mine the two-year batch logs using TGA-derived isoconversional kinetics of sunflower husks and particle-size decomposition data to shortlist the temperature–residence-time–particle-size combinations most likely to close the fixed-carbon gap without crossing the yield floor.
- Design a Box-Behnken response-surface experiment varying temperature (500–650°C), residence time, and particle size for both feedstocks and run the designed trials on the company's existing reactor, keeping all other equipment unchanged.
- Characterise every trial's biochar by proximate analysis (fixed carbon, volatile matter, ash) and BET surface area in the researcher's laboratory using the TGA-DSC, BET apparatus, FTIR and Raman spectrometers, and evaluate each output against European Biochar Certificate criteria.
- Fit the response-surface models, identify the process window that meets ≥70% fixed carbon and ≥20% yield, and confirm with three production-scale validation batches on the company's reactor.

### First six months
- Weeks 1–2: company provides a representative sample of sunflower husks and maize cobs and transfers two years of batch log files to the researcher under a signed NDA; researcher's master's student sets up a log-analysis spreadsheet and begins TGA runs.
- Weeks 3–6: master's student completes TGA kinetic analysis of both feedstocks in the researcher's lab; researcher uses the results to finalise the Box-Behnken design and agrees a reactor trial schedule with company staff.
- Months 2–4: company staff run ~15 designed trials per feedstock on the existing reactor; master's student travels to the company for at least three trial days and collects samples for lab characterisation back at the faculty.
- Months 4–6: master's student fits RSM models under researcher supervision; company staff run three validation batches per feedstock; researcher and company review results together and sign off on the final operating protocol.

### Why this pair
The company owns the reactor, the two-year logs and the exact feedstocks where the problem lives, but cannot close the fixed-carbon/yield gap because the experimental design method and characterisation lab are not available in-house. Vukelić brings response-surface optimisation experience on biochar temperature and residence time, isoconversional kinetics of sunflower husks, and a patent specifically on certified-grade sunflower-husk biochar — the precise technical combination the problem requires. Neither side can do it alone: the company needs the RSM methodology, kinetic analysis and characterisation equipment; the researcher needs the production reactor and feedstock at scale to produce results with direct commercial validity.

### Agreed up front (proposed)
- Publication: results may be submitted for publication after a 30-day review by the company; any specific process window identified as the optimum may be withheld from the paper at the company's written request.
- Ownership: the company owns the optimised operating protocol, the trial characterisation dataset and all outputs generated on its reactor; the researcher retains the right to use the RSM methodology, kinetic parameters and characterisation methods in future academic work.
- Data access: the company transfers batch logs to the researcher under a mutual NDA before trials begin; raw trial data is shared with both parties immediately after each run.
- Preconditions: the project starts only after the NDA is signed and the company confirms reactor availability for at least 15 trial runs within months 2–4.
