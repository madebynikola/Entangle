## Soil-Moisture Probe Lag Compensation for Apple Orchard Irrigation Control

**Company:** Described problem works in agriculture. [C999] Challenge C999-CH1.  
**Researcher:** Nenad Ilić is an Associate Professor in the Department of Field and Vegetable Crops, Faculty of Agriculture. [R0662]  
**Format:** joint project, one page.

### The problem
After rainfall the apple orchard irrigation controllers overwater because soil moisture probes react slowly, and a fixed pause after rainfall did not solve the problem (C999-CH1). [C999-CH1] A fixed pause inserted after rainfall did not solve the overwatering problem and young trees now show root stress (C999-CH1). [C999-CH1] The orchard covers 60 hectares and the company holds three seasons of probe readings, weather station data and yield records (C999-CH1). [C999-CH1]

### Prior work
- A 2018 study reports a dynamic neural network trained on past soil moisture, precipitation, and climatic measurements that predicts volumetric water content one day ahead with an R 2 value above 0.94 across three field sites, and achieves water savings of 20–46% compared with a rule-based threshold irrigation system. [W2896715775]
- A 2019 study reports an irrigation scheduling approach using capacitance probes for real-time soil water monitoring in a nectarine orchard, deriving irrigation factors from measured soil water deficits across the full growing season. [W2924508481]

### Research questions
1. Can a data-driven model trained on the three-season probe and weather archive predict post-rainfall volumetric water content at the probe depth accurately enough to replace the raw probe reading in the irrigation decision logic? Measured as RMSE and R² of predicted versus measured soil moisture on a held-out season, with a target R² above 0.90.
2. Does replacing the raw probe signal with the model's predicted moisture value reduce false irrigation events after rainfall compared with the current controller, without increasing root-stress indicators? Measured as the count of irrigation triggers within 24 hours of a rainfall event ≥5 mm, and soil moisture exceedance above field capacity, across the validation season.

### Approach
- Characterise the probe lag: use the three-season archive to quantify the time and magnitude of the gap between the probe reading and the weather-station-derived estimate of post-rain soil water content at each depth, applying the capacitive sensor calibration methods from the researcher's published work on multi-depth probe arrays.
- Build a predictive moisture model: train a dynamic model (feeding past probe readings, rainfall, temperature and ET₀ computed via Penman–Monteith) on two seasons of archive data to predict actual volumetric water content one step ahead, bypassing the lag period after rain.
- Develop a lag-aware decision rule: embed the model output into the irrigation feedback logic following the sensor-based decision framework the researcher published for precision irrigation, replacing the fixed-pause heuristic with a model-gated trigger that withholds irrigation when predicted moisture exceeds the target threshold.
- Validate in the field: run the new controller logic in parallel with the existing system during one growing season, logging both decisions and comparing false-trigger rates and soil moisture exceedance against the baseline.

### First six months
- Month 1 — Data transfer and audit: the company exports all three seasons of probe readings, weather station logs and yield records to the researcher's group; the master's student documents sensor placement, depths and soil type at each of the 60-ha monitoring points.
- Month 2 — Calibration: applying the researcher's multi-depth capacitive probe calibration protocol to the orchard soil type; the master's student and the company engineer take reference soil samples at representative orchard blocks to anchor the calibration.
- Month 3 — Lag characterisation: the master's student quantifies the post-rainfall probe lag (time to equilibration, magnitude by rainfall intensity band and depth) across the three-season archive; Nenad Ilić reviews outputs.
- Months 4–6 — Model development: the master's student trains the dynamic predictive moisture model on two seasons, validates it on the third, and drafts the lag-aware decision rule; the researcher and the company engineer review the rule against operational constraints before the irrigation season begins.

### Why this pair
The company has a well-defined sensor-lag problem and three seasons of data to solve it, but needs the calibration and decision-framework methods to turn that data into a working controller fix. Nenad Ilić brings exactly those methods: published capacitive probe calibration, a multi-depth probe patent, and a sensor-based irrigation decision framework. Neither side could do this alone: the company holds the orchard data and the live controller, while the researcher brings the calibration protocols and the decision logic that the model output must plug into.
